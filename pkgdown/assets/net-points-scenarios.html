Net Points — every credit and blame scenario

Every point of a match margin is allocated to the players on the field. This page walks through the eight kinds of play that get priced differently, each with real rows from the 2026 season. Companion to the player-level walkthroughs for a forward (Papley) and a defender (Andrews).

The one rule everything below serves: a play's value is the gap between the expected points before it and after it — delta_epv = (EV − before) + (after − EV). The first half is the decision: did this option beat what was expected, and it always goes to the player who chose it. The second half is the surprise: how far the actual outcome (kept, dropped, intercepted) landed from what the decision alone predicted, and it splits between the players involved.

Every row is charged twice: once as credit to the side that gained value, once as blame to the side that conceded it (torp 1.7.0, rating vintage v6). That's why each team's own players sum to that team's own margin, not just the gap between the two sides. Values below are in points, the same units as the final score.

Expected Points (EP)
The per-play state model — "if the match stopped right now, how many points is this position worth?" This is the exp_pts column, the "before" and "after" in every row-by-row trace below. It has nothing to do with EPV.
Net Points
What this whole page is about — the play-by-play EP swings, allocated to named players and re-summed so each team totals its own margin. This is the ledger.
EPV
An older, separate player rating (still published, not built from this ledger). Not used anywhere on this page — mentioned only so the two don't get confused.
Ruck stoppages

Centre bounces, ball-ups and boundary throw-ins

A stoppage is repriced against a neutral baseline for its type and location first — a centre bounce reads EP = 0 on the raw play-by-play, which would otherwise hide the whole swing. The ruck contest, the first player to gather, and a team-level pool each take a share. Two real sequences from the same match (Round 1, Sydney home to Carlton) below — one where the tap turns into a clean run, one where it turns into a scramble — and both end the same way, which is itself the point.

Single-stoppage swings to the ruck/first-possession roles are small — the biggest ruck-contest row in 75,646 stoppages this season is under one point. What moves the ledger is what happens on the next two or three plays, not the tap itself.

Shots

A kick that scores — and one that doesn't

A shot at goal is priced with the exact same decision/surprise split as any other kick — there is no separate "shot" rule. Two real passages below: one that finishes as a goal, one as a wasted behind, from the same round of 2026.

Uncontested receive

An uncontested mark

The receiver's share of the surprise term when the disposal lands cleanly and uncontested — value the receiving player earns just by being in space and taking the simple mark.

Contested receive

A contested mark

Winning a contested mark fires two separate terms on the same row: a contest-win credit for beating an opponent to it, and a receiver term that's still priced against how much of a coin-flip the contest was. Reading only one of the two — which the first version of this page did — understates or overstates what actually happened.

Other turnovers

Winning the ball back off a non-disposal act

Until 2026-09-07 the turnover split only fired when the lost ball came off a kick or a handball, so a loose-ball or hard-ball get that won the ball back paid the whole swing as blame to the side that lost it and credited the winner nothing (torp 1.6.0). Fixed: any act that loses the ball now pays its winner too.

Team pool

Value that belongs to a side, not a person

Every "team pool (bucket)" line in the examples above is banked to a team, not a player — it can't be pinned to one name at the moment it happens. Those buckets accumulate across the whole match and get spread across the roster once, at full time, by who was actually near the contests. Round 1, Sydney 132 d. Carlton 69 (a 63-point margin): Carlton's 23 listed players below all sum to exactly −63, their own team's margin.

"Pool share" here is always a cost to Carlton (their opponent's positive value against them) — it's what the pool spreads onto each Carlton player for value Sydney's players earned that couldn't be pinned to one Carlton defender. "Named" is everything each player earned directly, row by row, exactly like the examples above — note it doesn't rank the same as the final total: Sam Walsh's own play (−11.65) is worse than Ben Ainsworth's (+8.84), but Walsh's pool charge is smaller, so the gap between them narrows once the team's shared cost is spread on.

Downstream adjustments

What happens after the ledger balances

Two adjustments apply after the raw ledger above is built, and neither is a credit or blame rule in itself — they don't touch any of the row-by-row numbers shown on this page, only the separately published rating columns:

AdjustmentWhat it does
Position & opponent adjustment The published epv* columns are centred to zero and adjusted for position and opponent strength — they no longer sum to the match margin (2026: correlation 0.953 with the raw ledger, mean gap 10.3 points). For a final score, use the raw ledger or load_results(), not the published columns.
Conversion persistence Goal-kicking accuracy stays with the kicker rather than being pooled to the team — it repeats year to year (r 0.60 over 358 players) and predicts next year's conversion one-for-one, so it is treated as a real, keepable skill rather than noise to be spread around (D18).
Every number above is a real 2026 payment row, pulled straight from build_net_points().
